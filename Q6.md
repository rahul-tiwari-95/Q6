# Q6 — Mission, Research Program, and Roadmap

**Status:** Living document. Last updated 2026-07-27.
**Maintainer:** Rahul Tiwari.

---

## 1. Mission

Q6 studies a specific, reproducible failure mode in adversarial multi-agent reinforcement learning:

> When a reward function says "complete the objective" but the self-play training distribution says "the objective is dangerous," the agent learns to survive instead of completing the objective — because under the current opponent distribution, that is the mathematically correct thing to do.

We call this **policy bias toward the risk-dominant equilibrium**. It is not a bug. It is the agent behaving rationally under incentives the training process created. The game (Kṛṣṇa collects pellets in a 25×25 gridworld, Hunter chases) is deliberately small and fully legible so the phenomenon can be isolated, measured, and fixed in the open — not buried inside a benchmark too large for any one person to fully instrument.

Q6 is not attempting to invent new RL algorithms. Every individual component (Double DQN, Dueling networks, Fictitious Self-Play, HER-style relabeling, PPO) is textbook. The value of Q6 is the combination, the instrumentation, the documented history of what didn't work and why, and the fact that all of it is reproducible on a laptop or a single rented GPU.

## 2. Vision

Q6 becomes a small, open, continuously-published research program rather than a finished artifact — a place where:

1. Every hypothesis is written down *before* it's tested (see `versions/`), and every result is written down *after*, including negative ones.
2. The code, the training logs, the dashboard, and the write-ups are all public and reproducible by a stranger with a GPU.
3. The research questions stay connected to things people actively publish about in 2025-2026 (risk dominance / payoff dominance, strategy collapse in self-play, plasticity loss, non-stationary MARL) rather than drifting into an idiosyncratic private language.
4. It attracts collaborators who want to run their own ablation, not just read about ours — the repo is structured so "here's a cheap, well-specified experiment, go run it" is always available as an on-ramp.

The long-run bet: a small, rigorously-documented, honestly-negative-results-included research thread on this exact phenomenon does not currently exist in accessible form anywhere (confirmed via literature/landscape search, July 2026 — see §8). The topic is real, decades of game theory back the framing, and 2025-2026 papers are actively working on adjacent formalizations. Nobody has claimed the "legible, reproducible, open version" of this story yet.

## 3. Research Questions

### 3.1 Primary — Policy bias in adversarial self-play

> Can a learned policy balance mission progress, survival, exploration, and adversarial adaptation when the opponent is also learning — or does it reliably collapse to the risk-dominant, task-incomplete equilibrium?

Status: **open, actively tested**. Every version (v1→v7) has rediscovered some form of this. See §6.

### 3.2 Secondary — Continual learning as plasticity retention

> Independent of opponent strength or reward design, does the network's capacity to keep learning degrade over the course of a long, non-stationary self-play run — and can that degradation be measured and mitigated?

This is the precise version of "figure out continual learning" that Q6 is committing to (see §5 for why the other four candidate framings were scoped out). It is a training-dynamics question, orthogonal to §3.1, that happens to co-occur with it: every Fictitious Self-Play opponent-pool swap is a non-stationary distribution shift of exactly the kind the plasticity-loss literature studies.

Status: **open, not yet instrumented**. No plasticity diagnostics currently exist in the codebase.

### 3.3 Tertiary, active as of v8 — Is the collapse algorithm-specific or structural?

> Does the risk-dominant collapse in §3.1 persist if Krishna and Hunter are trained with PPO/IPPO instead of DQN — or is it an artifact of DQN's off-policy replay buffer compounding opponent non-stationarity?

This is the current bet (branch `v8`, started 2026-07-27). If the same collapse shows up under PPO, that's strong evidence the phenomenon is a property of the *game's payoff structure*, not of Q-learning — a materially stronger scientific claim than anything Q6 has produced so far, and the flagship result for the publication plan in §7.

### 3.4 How We Got Here — A Narrated Walkthrough

*The following is written by Claude, narrating the investigation Rahul and I worked through together over one long conversation in July 2026 — not a transcript, not quoted dialogue, but the shape of the reasoning, the wrong turns, and the literature we pulled in along the way. If you're reading Q6 for the first time, this section is meant to let you reconstruct the thinking rather than just receive the conclusions.*

**On policy bias and risk dominance**

The way into this project wasn't the code. It was a number. Rahul had a `v7` branch sitting untouched for about five weeks — a training run that had implemented exactly what the project's own `v6` plan called for: penalize timing out with zero pellets, make the proximity shaping conditional on the Hunter being far away, add a regularizer to stop the evade/collect gate from collapsing to pure evasion, weight the synthetic CHER teaching signal by how safe the moment actually was. Every change was individually defensible. The run took thirty-six hours. When it finished, Krishna had won zero of twelve thousand games, and the average reward had spent almost the entire run in negative territory — worse, on every metric that mattered, than the version it was supposed to improve on.

That's the kind of result that makes a person quietly stop opening a repo. Not because anything crashed, but because the *reasoning* seemed to fail. You do the diagnosis correctly, you implement the fix the diagnosis calls for, and the patient gets sicker. What do you even trust after that?

The first move was to stop treating "v7 didn't work" as one fact and start treating it as a claim that needed to survive being poked at. Reading the actual diff against `main` line by line turned up something the run's own summary metrics couldn't have shown: the "weighted CHER" change — the one meant to make the synthetic collection-teaching signal *stronger* when the Hunter was farther away — used a formula (`bonus = 30 × min(2.0, hunter_dist / 12)`) that only reaches parity with the old flat bonus once the Hunter is twelve squares away. But CHER only fires once the Hunter crosses six squares away in the first place, and a Hunter that's actively hunting is going to spend most of its "just barely safe" moments in the seven-to-eleven range, not way out past twelve. For the common case, the new "improvement" was quietly a weaker signal than what it replaced — landing in the same commit that made the live penalty for *not* collecting harsher. Two changes, each reasonable alone, working against each other.

That reframes the whole result. It's not "the hypothesis that reward asymmetry causes the collapse was wrong." It's "the hypothesis was never cleanly tested, because the thing doing most of the teaching in the previous version got quietly weakened in the same run that was supposed to test it." That distinction matters enormously for what to do next — it's the difference between abandoning a research direction and re-running a cleaner version of the same experiment.

But the deeper question underneath the bug is the more interesting one, and it's the one that pulled in outside literature: is this *kind* of thing — an agent discovering that the safe, incomplete strategy beats the risky, correct one — something the field already has a name for? The honest answer turned out to be "several partial names, no single one." Game theory has had the cleanest version of it since 1988: Harsanyi and Selten's distinction between a *payoff-dominant* equilibrium (better for everyone if you can coordinate on it) and a *risk-dominant* one (safer under uncertainty about what the other player will do, and so more likely to be where training actually lands). The Stag Hunt is the canonical illustration — two hunters can catch a stag together for a big payoff, or each can catch a hare alone for a smaller but guaranteed one, and if either hunter doubts the other will commit, hunting hare is the rational choice even though it's worse for both of them if they'd coordinated. Krishna's "just survive" strategy is a hare. Collecting all four pellets against a hardened Hunter is the stag. Nothing about Krishna's evasion is a malfunction — it's risk-dominant reasoning, done correctly, in a game that happens to reward something else if only both players could get there.

AlphaStar's own paper names an adjacent failure "strategy collapse," and builds an entire League — main agents, main exploiters, league exploiters — specifically so naive self-play doesn't settle into a narrow, defensible strategy and stay there. That's structurally close to what a hierarchical opponent pool with a permanent easy tier is trying to do for Krishna, just at a much smaller scale.

The most specific match came from a paper barely two months old at the time of this conversation — "The Attacker in the Mirror" (arXiv:2605.08427), which proves that certain self-play Nash equilibria in an AI-safety red-teaming setup can be "trivial always-refuse" strategies: safe, defensible, and completely useless, with the same formal game value as a genuinely competent defender. That's a remarkably precise mirror of "Krishna times out safely instead of collecting." But precision at the level of the *symptom* isn't the same as precision at the level of the *mechanism*, and it was worth being honest about the difference rather than reaching for the closest-sounding citation and calling it a day. Their failure comes from the attacker and defender roles sharing parameters — updating the same weights pulls both roles toward mutual accommodation, a "self-consistency" collapse that a first-order Taylor expansion of the coupled gradients makes visible. Krishna and Hunter don't share any parameters; they're separate networks from the start. So the mechanism that paper diagnoses doesn't transfer literally. What *does* transfer is the shape of their fix — freeze something as an anchor, and let the two roles diverge from that fixed point instead of drifting into each other. Q6's hierarchical pool already does a rough version of this with its permanent easy-tier opponents. The paper suggests a more principled version: anchor to a frozen *reference policy*, not just to easy opponents, which is where the frozen-collect-head-anchor ablation in §6.1 came from.

**On what "continual learning" actually means here**

Rahul's original framing for the whole project, going back to before any of this, was simpler than all of the above: figure out continual learning. That phrase is generous enough to mean five genuinely different research problems, and the honest thing to do before building anything was to lay all five out rather than pick the one that sounded good.

Two didn't fit at all, once stated precisely. Open-ended learning — POET, PAIRED, the unsupervised-environment-design lineage — is about a system that *generates* increasingly novel and learnable environments; Q6's opponent pool only ever replays policies that already existed, so it's the wrong axis entirely. Classical lifelong or multi-task RL assumes an agent that has to stay competent across a *sequence* of qualitatively different tasks over its life; Q6 has exactly one game. Citing either would have been reaching for prestige the project hadn't earned.

Two did fit, but they're not the same claim, and it was worth keeping them separate rather than blurring them into one vague thread. The first is what the self-play literature is already quietly doing under a different name: fictitious self-play, opponent pools, league training — all of it is a response to a non-stationary opponent, and a 2024 survey of the whole self-play literature (arXiv:2408.01072) uses the word "non-stationarity" throughout its introduction and never once says "continual learning." That's telling. It means Q6's FSP pool is *already* a continual-learning technique in substance, just not in the vocabulary the field around it uses — and there's exactly one paper found in the search, Kaplanis, Shanahan & Clopath's "Policy Consolidation for Continual RL" (ICML 2019), that explicitly frames competitive self-play this way and is worth citing precisely for that reason.

The second is a genuinely different, more classical question: independent of the opponent, does the network itself lose the *capacity* to keep learning after tens of thousands of episodes of non-stationary gradient updates? This is plasticity loss — Dohare et al.'s 2024 *Nature* paper showed standard backprop networks can degrade toward performing no better than a much shallower network after prolonged non-stationary training, and Nikishin et al.'s "primacy bias" work showed deep RL agents specifically overfit to their early replay-buffer experience in a way that periodic partial resets can measurably fix. This is the one Rahul picked, and it's a good pick for Q6 specifically: it's measurable with off-the-shelf diagnostics (dormant-neuron ratios, effective rank, weight-norm growth), it doesn't require redesigning the game or the reward function, and — usefully — it's completely agnostic to whether the underlying learner is DQN or PPO, so it can run as its own thread alongside the v8 work rather than being blocked by it.

**On why the algorithm itself became a question, not just the reward**

The last thread worth narrating is the one that turned this from "keep patching the reward function" into "test whether the algorithm is even the right instrument." DQN's replay buffer, sitting at a hundred thousand transitions, holds experience generated against Hunter policies from thousands of episodes ago — Hunters that, in a meaningful sense, no longer exist. Q-learning's target computation implicitly treats every sampled transition as if it came from the same stationary process. In self-play, it very much didn't: the opponent *is* part of the environment's dynamics as far as Krishna's Q-function is concerned, and nothing in vanilla Double DQN corrects for the fact that the environment has been quietly rewritten thousands of times since half the buffer was collected. Fictitious Self-Play patches this at the level of "which opponent do I play this episode" — but the replay buffer itself doesn't know or care which Hunter-version produced a given transition; it pools everything together as if it were one process.

On-policy methods sidestep this by construction: PPO collects a batch of trajectories from the *current* policy pair, updates on exactly that batch, and throws it away. The staleness window shrinks from "the entire training history" to "the last rollout." That's not a fashion preference — it's a specific, mechanical reason to expect PPO self-play to behave differently here, and it converts an assumption ("DQN is fine for this") into an experiment ("does the collapse survive the algorithm change"). Structurally, since Krishna and Hunter are adversaries rather than teammates, there's no shared value function to centralize the way MAPPO does for cooperative agents — independent PPO, one actor-critic per agent, matches the zero-sum structure of the game, and has real precedent in exactly this kind of setting (Bansal et al.'s "Emergent Complexity via Multi-Agent Competition").

But it was worth resisting the temptation to treat "switch to PPO" as a fix. It removes one specific confound — buffer staleness — and nothing more. The risk-dominance mechanism described above doesn't care what algorithm is doing the learning; it's a property of the game's payoff structure under opponent uncertainty. If v8 shows the same collapse under PPO, that's not a failure of the port. It's the cleanest possible evidence that Q6 has been looking at something structural all along, not an artifact of the specific learning rule — which, if it holds, is a better and more publishable result than a fix would have been.

## 4. Current Status (2026-07-27)

The project was paused for about five weeks after the `v7` training run (`20260618_230700_phase3_v7_reward_fix`, 2026-06-19 → 06-20, 12,000 episodes, 36.7 hours) came back **worse than v5 on every headline metric** despite implementing the reward-asymmetry fix that v6's plan had called for:

| Metric | v5 (GOP+CHER) | v7 (reward redesign) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Krishna win rate | 2 / 12,000 | **0 / 12,000** |
| Timeout rate | 97.8% | 70.6% (down, but replaced by Hunter wins, not Krishna wins) |
| Hunter win rate | 2.2% | **29.4%** |
| Late avg100 | +8.58 | **negative throughout** (-14 to -37, ending ≈ -17) |
| Pellet-touch rate, early → late | n/a | **40.5% → 21.7% (declined)** |
| Gate (evade-leaning = higher) | ends at 0.545 | ends at 0.63, trending up |

Root-cause analysis (this document's own investigation, 2026-07-27) found this is likely **not** a clean falsification of v6's hypothesis. Three changes landed in the same commit (`cb5153b`): a harsher live penalty for zero-pellet timeouts (`K_TIMEOUT_ZERO_PELLETS = -20`), a *weighted* CHER bonus, and gate-entropy regularization. The weighted-CHER formula (`bonus = 30 × min(2.0, hunter_dist / 12)`) gives a **weaker** synthetic-teaching signal than v5's flat +30 for `hunter_dist` in the 7–11 range — plausibly the most common range for real opportunities, since CHER only fires just past the safety threshold of 6. So the run may have simultaneously punished the safe strategy harder *and* weakened the mechanism that was teaching the risky one — three reasonable changes working against each other, not one bad hypothesis.

**This is not yet written up in `versions/`.** Writing `versions/v7_reward_redesign.md` documenting this finding is a recommended near-term task (see §6.1).

## 5. How We're Approaching Continual Learning

"Continual learning" is overloaded across five distinct RL sub-problems. We deliberately scope Q6's use of the term to avoid the mistake of borrowing prestige from a phrase without doing the precise version of the work.

| Framing | Verdict for Q6 | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasticity loss / primacy bias** (Dohare et al., *Nature* 2024; Nikishin et al., ICML 2022) | **Adopted — this is what we mean** | Directly measurable, directly caused by the non-stationarity Q6 already has (opponent pool swaps), doesn't require redesigning the game. |
| **Non-stationary-opponent continual RL** (self-play/FSP framed as anti-forgetting) | **Adopted as framing, not as a new experiment** | This is the literature Q6's existing FSP + hierarchical pool already lives in — it's just not usually *called* continual learning (the field's own self-play survey, arXiv:2408.01072, never uses the phrase). We'll use this vocabulary to describe what the pool already does, and cite Kaplanis et al. (ICML 2019) as the one paper that explicitly frames competitive self-play this way. |
| Streaming RL without episodic resets | Noted, not pursued now | A genuinely different axis (removing replay buffers / reset conventions entirely) — interesting, possibly a later thread once plasticity diagnostics exist, not a near-term priority. |
| Open-ended learning / UED (POET, PAIRED) | **Out of scope** | Wrong axis — about *generating* novel environments/curricula, not about retention. Q6's pool replays past policies, it doesn't invent new ones. |
| Classical lifelong/multi-task RL (sequential distinct tasks) | **Out of scope** | Category error — assumes a task *sequence*. Q6 has one fixed game. |

**Concrete plan:** instrument standard plasticity diagnostics — dormant-neuron ratio, effective rank of hidden representations, weight-norm growth, gradient-norm decay (per Lyle et al., arXiv:2402.18762) — into the training loop, logged alongside existing per-episode stats, and correlate against opponent-pool transition events. If plasticity measurably degrades over a 12k-episode run, test a continual-backprop-style intervention (reinitializing low-utility units) or periodic partial resets (Nikishin et al.) as a mitigation. This is backbone-agnostic — it applies whether the underlying learner is DQN or PPO, so it can run alongside the v8 PPO work rather than blocking it.

### 6.1 Immediate open threads (not yet run)

Two cheap, targeted ablations were identified (2026-07-27) that could resolve the v7 ambiguity without another multi-day combined-change run — candidates for whoever picks up the DQN side of the project next, in parallel with v8's PPO work:

1. **Rectified opponent sampling** (`utils/hierarchical_pool.py`, ~1 file) — weight FSP snapshot sampling by a PSRO-rectified score (train more against opponents you're currently beating, not just the hardest ones), instead of the fixed `p_easy=0.25 / p_latest=0.7` split.
2. **Frozen-policy anchor for the collect head** (`agent/gated_dqn_agent.py`, ~1 loss term) — regularize the collect head toward a frozen Phase-1 checkpoint (needs regenerating — none currently exists on disk) in safe states, decayed to 0 over the first few thousand episodes, to test whether collection is being actively *forgotten* under adversarial pressure rather than never learned.

Success criterion for both: `cf_dependency` (already logged) declines below 0.2 in the last 2,000 episodes — something no run has achieved yet. A negative result on both is itself informative: it would mean the bottleneck really is the live reward asymmetry, not a training-distribution/equilibrium-selection problem.

## 7. Publication Plan

Format guidance from the landscape research (§8): **one falsifiable finding per post**, code permalinked next to each specific claim, plots that isolate one variable at a time, an explicit "what didn't work" section, stated hardware/compute/cost, plain non-clickbait titles. Short, frequent research notes beat marathon posts for sustaining a solo audience — this maps directly onto the discipline already in `versions/`.

Draft article sequence (not a commitment to write all of these — a menu, ordered roughly by how ready the material already is):

1. **"I built a tiny predator-prey RL game and it keeps rediscovering game theory."** Narrative intro through v1→v7. The honest, human version of the story — including stopping for five weeks after v7 came back worse — is more compelling than a cleaned-up success story, and sets up every later post.
2. **"I fixed the bug, ran it for 36 hours, and it got worse."** The v7 postmortem: the weighted-CHER-vs-timeout-penalty confound, and the two cheap ablations proposed to disentangle it. A genuinely useful post about debugging RL experiments, not just Q6-specific.
3. **"Is my agent's cowardice a Q-learning bug or a Nash equilibrium?"** The v8 flagship: same risk-dominance diagnostic run under both DQN self-play and PPO self-play. If the collapse shows up under both, that's the strongest, most citable claim Q6 has produced — connects directly to the risk-dominance / payoff-dominance literature (Harsanyi & Selten) and to 2025-2026 papers actively working the same territory (arXiv:2305.11476, 2405.02724, 2603.09208, 2605.18078).
4. **"Making a 25×25 gridworld fast enough to not care."** The GPU-vectorization engineering piece — appeals to a different (more infra-flavored) audience, and the vectorized env is itself a reusable open-source artifact independent of the research findings.
5. **"Does my agent forget how to learn?"** The plasticity-loss / continual-learning piece (§3.2, §5) — diagnostics, methodology, findings.
6. Later, contingent on what's found: league/PSRO-style opponent diversity experiments, or a reward-redesign-v2 if the DQN-side ablations in §6.1 point that way.

## 8. Building Visibility and Community

**Confirmed gap** (literature/landscape search, July 2026): no solo blog, Substack, or maintained repo currently treats "self-play converges to a risk-dominant safe policy instead of the payoff-dominant objective" as an ongoing, open, code-first thread. The phenomenon is real and academically active (Harsanyi & Selten's risk-dominance game theory, plus several 2023-2026 MARL papers on equilibrium bias under self-play), but it lives exclusively in dense peer-reviewed papers. The closest model for tone and rigor is Andy Jones (`andyljones.com`, "Scaling Scaling Laws with Board Games," single RTX 2080 Ti, log-log rigor) — worth noting honestly that he subsequently joined Anthropic, i.e. the closest precedent for this kind of solo work became a hiring signal rather than an indefinitely sustained independent thread. That's a fine outcome either way.

Concrete tactics:

- **Distribution:** Substack as primary channel; one-chart-one-sentence teasers on X/Twitter linking back rather than retelling the whole post in-thread; submit to Hacker News with a plain, literal title (not clickbait — this audience penalizes it).
- **Communities:** r/reinforcementlearning, r/MachineLearning; the LessWrong / Alignment Forum "model organisms" crowd is a strong fit given the specification-gaming / real-world-analogue framing already in `Qsix_current_policy_biases.mdx` (agents avoiding useful-but-risky tasks is directly relevant to their interests); EleutherAI's Discord and "Research Notes" culture is the closest existing community norm for honest small negative-result writeups.
- **Engage the literature directly:** the 2025-2026 papers on equilibrium bias in self-play (arXiv:2305.11476, 2405.02724, 2603.09208, 2605.18078) and the self-consistency-collapse paper (arXiv:2605.08427) are being written by people working this exact problem right now. Citing them properly, and where relevant reaching out, is a legitimate way to be found by people already interested — citation-graph visibility, not just social-media visibility.
- **Repo hygiene as an on-ramp for collaborators:** a license file, a short CONTRIBUTING note, and a handful of issues tagged "good first experiment" (the two ablations in §6.1 are ready-made candidates) turn "interesting blog post" readers into people who can actually run something. Tagged releases matching version numbers, and fixing the reproducibility gap already flagged in `Qsix_current_policy_biases.mdx` (every run should record git commit + dirty-worktree state + exact CLI args — partially done as of v7, not yet universal) make the repo trustworthy to build on.
- **The dashboard is an underused asset.** It's already a static HTML/JS/JSON app (`dashboard/`) — deploying it publicly (GitHub Pages or similar) gives people something visual and explorable to link to, distinct from a wall of markdown.
- **Cadence over intensity.** Short, frequent research notes (roughly 1× per 1-2 weeks) sustain a solo technical audience better than sporadic long posts — consistent with the discipline `versions/` already has.

## 9. Branching Strategy

Going forward, branches track active research bets; `main` tracks only conclusions that have a written version log and updated dashboard data behind them — the same pattern that already happened for Phase 1 + Phase 2.

- **`main`** — the current best documented understanding of Q6. Merge into it only once a thread has a `versions/vN_*.md` write-up and (if it produced runs) dashboard data.
- **`vN` branches** — one per major architectural/algorithmic bet. `v7` (reward redesign) and `v8` (PPO port, starting now) follow this pattern. A `vN` branch can be negative-result-and-abandoned — that's fine, it stays in history and gets written up in `versions/` regardless of outcome.
- Individual training runs within a branch continue to be tracked the existing way: thesis → architecture → results → failure mode → decision, one file per version under `versions/`.
